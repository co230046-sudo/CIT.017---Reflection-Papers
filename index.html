Security in Depth: Uncovering the Layers of System Protection

David John J. De Jesus · 4th Year BSIT · August 2026·3 min read

the McCumber Cube · Authentication, Authorization & Accounting · the CIA Triad · Principles of Security

❖

💡 Tip: click any paragraph to highlight it

Coming into this lesson I thought I already had a decent grasp on information security. As an IT student I always assumed security simply meant writing bug-free code to keep a system running. However delving into these core frameworks made me realize that security is rarely about the system itself, it is fundamentally about protecting the users and their data. Security is never a simple black and white subject. It is a deeply layered discipline and analyzing these models completely reshaped how I view my role as a future developer.

The McCumber Cube was very helpful in reshaping my perspective. It showed me that securing a system requires us to evaluate everything happening both inside and outside of a system. At first I thought the model felt confusing and overwhelming because it forces us to consider Information States, Security Goals, and Countermeasures all at once. However I realized that these core factors serve as the foundation for what to consider when developing a system. Before studying Information States I mostly thought about data when it sat in a database. Realizing that data in transmission, storage, or processing carries its own unique vulnerabilities made me rethink how data moves through an entire application. Security Goals shifted my focus to the user side through confidentiality, integrity, and availability. Countermeasures forced me to look beyond just the tech and evaluate how organizational policies or human behavior might compromise our system. What made me so interested in the McCumber Cube was this realization that how data is handled across these dimensions can actually be a vulnerability on its own.

When it comes to the Foundations of Security the Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting framework was the most helpful for my learning. I did not realize before that authentication and authorization had completely different goals. Learning about authentication gave me more insight because I learned that proving identity goes beyond simple password fields to include things like biometrics or OTPs. This directly reinforced how I build login pages and how I should treat users on a website. Concepts like Prevention, Detection, and Mitigation were also eye-opening. I used to focus almost entirely on prevention when building projects, but learning about detection and mitigation made me realize I had no plan for what happens when a system inevitably gets compromised. The lessons also deepened my understanding of integrity and availability in real life scenarios beyond just their definitions. A good example I realized is that integrity means keeping an accurate bank balance while availability means ensuring critical systems stay working in tough situations like accessing medical records in hospitals.

The security principles were very insightful especially Separation of Duty, Security by Design, and the Principle of Complete Mediation. These three really opened my eyes to how we can make a system more secure. I used to think securing a system was mostly about writing technical code but now I see it is really about how you scope and design the architecture from the start. Principles are the foundation beneath any technical coding implementation. The scenario about the municipal payroll and its lack of auditing and clear roles highlighted these principles perfectly. It demonstrated real life vulnerabilities caused by weak design which will help me scope and implement security much better in my Capstone and future projects.

— D.J. De Jesus
